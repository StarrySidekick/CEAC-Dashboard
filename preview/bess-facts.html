CEAC · Battery Energy Storage

The battery is a parking space. The setback is the project.

Every siting argument in Westchester is framed as an argument about how big batteries are. It is not. At the three sizes a town actually regulates, the equipment ranges from a stretch of garage wall to a single parking space. What consumes the land is the rule the town writes around it.

Where each number comes from

Three different kinds of authority get called "the setback rule," and they are not interchangeable. State fire code is binding everywhere. The NYSERDA model law is a template. A neighbouring town's local law is that town's own choice.

State fire code

600 kWh is a fire code line, not a size class

The 2020 Fire Code of New York State sets a Maximum Allowable Quantity of 600 kWh of lithium-ion storage per fire area (Table 1206.12), and a 20 kWh threshold at which Section 1206 applies at all (Table 1206.1).

2020 FCNYS §1206, Tables 1206.1 and 1206.12

Standard

NFPA 855 governs the gaps between units

Three feet between individual units, and three feet from doors and windows, unless UL 9540A large-scale fire testing and the authority having jurisdiction support closer spacing. This is a clearance, not a property-line setback.

NFPA 855, unit spacing provisions

Model template

The NYSERDA model law sets no Tier 2 number

It states that Tier 2 systems "shall comply with the setback requirements of the underlying zoning district for principal structures." It brackets one distance as a suggested default: ten feet of vegetation clearance on each side. The 200 ft figure in the model law is a mailed-notice radius, not a setback.

NYSERDA BESS Model Law

Local choice

Adopted local laws range from 50 ft to 1,000 ft

Cambria and Wheatfield (2022): 50 ft non-residential, 100 ft residential. West Seneca (2025): 100 ft non-residential, 500 ft from a residential district line, 1,000 ft from schools, hospitals and waterways. Wheatfield adds a local "Tier 3" at 500 to 1,000 ft.

NY local laws, ecode360

CEAC · Battery Energy Storage

A battery site and a house have about the same odds of catching fire this year.

That is the whole comparison, and it survives a fact-check. Every figure on this page carries its numerator, its denominator and its year, because a risk number without a denominator is not a risk number. The last section lists the figures we found and deliberately did not use.

What we cannot say

Three incidents against 57 inspected sites is not a rate. A rate needs total statewide site-years, which no single primary source publishes. Do not divide 3 by 57 and present the result. The defensible statement is the count and the outcome, not a computed percentage.

Separately, the figure "5,000-plus battery storage projects in New York" circulates widely. It almost certainly folds in small residential systems and cannot be paired with the 57 utility-scale figure without producing a false rate.

Moss Landing, handled honestly

The single event most likely to be raised in opposition. Conceding what is true about it is more persuasive than talking around it.

What happened

January 2025, Monterey County, California

A fire at Vistra's Elkhorn Battery facility. The site flared again in September 2026, more than a year and a half later, which indicates the underlying issue was not resolved quickly.

CalMatters, 18 September 2026

Why it is an outlier

Older chemistry, indoor configuration

Reporting attributes its severity to nickel-manganese-cobalt cells in a dense, building-scale indoor arrangement. New US utility-scale projects are overwhelmingly lithium iron phosphate in outdoor containerised enclosures, which fail less energetically and do not propagate through a shared building volume.

MIT Climate Portal and industry technical summaries

What is not settled

No official cause report was located

No finalised Cal Fire, CPUC or Monterey County cause-and-origin report was found. Do not attribute a specific official finding to anyone. A peer-reviewed paper did document cathode-metal deposition in nearby wetlands from this fire.

CEAC · Battery Energy Storage

Twenty years of a battery, start to finish.

Ron asked us to document that storage is cleaner. The version worth publishing is not the one that wins every stage. It is the one that concedes the two stages where the case is genuinely weak, because those are the stages an informed opponent will name first.

The five stages

Select a stage. Timings are for a utility-scale lithium iron phosphate system, the chemistry in the large majority of new grid storage.

Lithium, iron, phosphate, graphite. No cobalt, no nickel.

Lithium iron phosphate is the chemistry that took over grid storage, and its composition is the substantive part of the environmental argument. The cathode is LiFePO₄, an iron and phosphate compound on aluminium foil. The anode is graphite on copper foil. There is a liquid organic electrolyte, a polymer separator, and a steel or aluminium enclosure.

Why the absence matters

Cobalt and nickel are where the documented human-rights and ecological harms of battery supply chains concentrate: artisanal cobalt mining in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, nickel laterite processing in Indonesia. LFP does not use either. That is a real advantage and it is specific, not rhetorical.

What it does not solve

Lithium extraction remains real. Andean brine evaporation and hard-rock mining in Australia and Nevada carry documented water use, land disturbance and local ecological impacts. The honest framing is that LFP avoids the worst-documented hotspots, not that it has no mining footprint.

The manufacturing footprint is front-loaded and grid-dependent.

Cell manufacturing is energy intensive, and its carbon intensity depends heavily on the electricity mix where the factory sits. A cell made on a coal-heavy grid carries materially higher embodied carbon than the same cell made on a clean one. This is the stage where a battery's emissions are concentrated, and it happens once.

The shape of the comparison

A battery's footprint is front-loaded, then amortised across roughly two decades and thousands of cycles of zero-combustion operation. A gas peaker's footprint is continuous: it emits every hour it runs, for its whole life, in the same place.

Do not publish a number here yet

We could not verify a single citable primary figure for grams of CO₂e per delivered kWh comparing storage plus renewables against a gas peaker. The relevant peer-reviewed study does not produce that number; it finds carbon pricing rather than storage technology is the dominant lever, with storage manufacturing at 1 to 3 percent of total system emissions in its scenarios. Generating a defensible figure means running NREL's harmonised dataset or Argonne's GREET model directly.

Twenty years, with augmentation around year ten. Not a replacement.

This is the correction worth making before anything goes out. The brief described a roughly ten-year cell replacement interval. The industry norm is augmentation: adding capacity incrementally to offset degradation, rather than tearing out and replacing the battery. PNNL models this as roughly 30 percent of power equipment cost per kWh incurred every ten years. Systems are engineered with headroom for it and the hardware is modular by design.

What twenty years actually means

Twenty years is an industry convention, not a fixed constant. Fluence describes twenty years or 60 percent remaining capacity as the practical end-of-life point. PNNL's 2022 cost assessment models a thirty-year project life. Its 2019 predecessor used ten years, reflecting pre-LFP assumptions. Say "commonly cited as twenty years" rather than "the design life."

Commonly cited LFP cycle life is 6,000 cycles at 80 percent depth of discharge, or around 8,000 cycles at a half-C rate, landing at 70 to 80 percent state of health.

What vendors actually warrant

Tesla warrants the Megapack 2 XL for 15 years, extendable to 20, all-inclusive on equipment and energy retention. CATL's grid-scale Tianheng product carries a 20-year capacity-retention warranty with a 5-year zero-degradation guarantee.

Do not reuse CATL's automotive warranty terms here. Those are a different product with different numbers.

Bonded before it is built, which the plant it replaces usually is not.

The NYSERDA model law requires a decommissioning plan covering the activities and who performs them, hazardous and solid waste disposal, the anticipated system life, a cost estimate with its methodology, and a site restoration plan. Separately it requires the owner to continuously maintain a fund or bond payable to the municipality for the removal of the system, for the life of the facility, with all costs borne by the applicant.

The asymmetry is the argument

Resources for the Future finds that fossil plants in deregulated markets often face no binding financial-assurance requirement at all: no local, state or federal policy ensures adequate decommissioning funding. Renewables and storage increasingly face mandatory pre-construction bonding. Batteries are being held to a stricter standard than the peakers they displace.

Cost, with the caveat attached

One published project-level example puts a 120 MW site at roughly 6 million dollars total, about 50,000 dollars per MW. RFF puts natural gas plants at about 15,000 dollars per MW on average, from 28 estimates in 2016 dollars. These are different years and different scopes, so this is a rough bracket, not a like-for-like comparison. The bonding point above is the sturdier one.

Technically solved, financially fragile. Say so.

This is the weakest stage of the case and the one an opponent will find. Two of the highest-profile US battery recyclers collapsed within about a year of each other. Li-Cycle filed for bi-national bankruptcy in May 2025; Glencore acquired its assets that August, and only its German plant operates while every North American facility sits idle, including the 485 million dollar Rochester hub that never opened. Ascend Elements filed Chapter 11 in April 2026 after a 316 million dollar federal grant was cancelled mid-disbursement.

What is working

Redwood Materials remains the strongest domestic player, states it recovers more than 95 percent of the critical elements in batteries, and receives roughly 6 GWh of lithium batteries a year. Its Nevada site pairs 63 MWh of second-life EV batteries with 20 MW of solar. Second-life batteries are about 2 to 3 percent of deployed US grid storage today, projected at 20 to 25 percent within a decade.

The 95 percent figure is a company claim, combined across elements rather than broken out, and not independently audited. Attribute it.

Two real problems

LFP is the hardest chemistry to recycle profitably. Its material value runs roughly four to five times lower than nickel or cobalt chemistries, because the valuable metals are exactly the ones LFP omits. The same property that makes it cleaner to mine makes it less worth recovering.

New York's battery recycling law does not cover grid storage. The Rechargeable Battery Recycling Act explicitly excludes batteries for storage of electricity from an alternative power source, and anything weighing 50 pounds or more. Federally, spent lithium batteries are likely hazardous waste on ignitability and reactivity, manageable under the Universal Waste programme, with a rulemaking still pending.

The four places this argument is weakest

Collected in one place so they can be answered on purpose rather than discovered in a Q&A.

1. Recycling is financially fragile, not mature

Two major US recyclers failed inside a year, driven by soft EV demand and withdrawn federal funding rather than by failed technology. Do not imply a bankable recycling industry stands ready. Say the technology works, the economics are unsettled, and re-check recycler status shortly before anything is published, because this is moving.

2. LFP recycling economics are genuinely poor

No cobalt and no nickel means little worth recovering. Recycling LFP today depends on regulation, subsidy, or blending with higher-value feedstock, not on standalone market economics. This is the direct cost of the chemistry choice that makes LFP cleaner and safer. Own the trade-off rather than presenting only its upside.

3. New York law has a grid-storage gap

The state's Rechargeable Battery Recycling Act does not cover the batteries in question. Anyone who reads the statute will find this. Better to name it as a gap worth closing than to be shown it. It is also a concrete, constructive ask the coalition could carry to Albany.

4. There is no verified lifecycle carbon figure in this draft

We deliberately left the headline grams of CO₂e per kWh blank rather than borrow an unsourced number. That is the number this visual most wants and the one most likely to be wrong. Producing it properly means running NREL's harmonised emissions-factor dataset or Argonne's GREET model.
